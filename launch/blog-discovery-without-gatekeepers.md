# A directory should introduce you, then get out of the way

Imagine finding a plumber through a neighbourhood directory.

The directory helps you compare services, check availability and choose someone nearby. You send an enquiry. The plumber replies, you agree on the work, and a pipe eventually stops doing something expensive.

The directory has done a useful job. It can charge for that job.

Now imagine that continuing the conversation requires its inbox, keeping the quote requires its storage, and finding the plumber again requires maintaining an account in its system. An introduction has become a permanent tenancy.

A large part of the internet bundles discovery with ownership of everything that happens next. I want to explore what happens when we separate those responsibilities.

## Publish once, appear in several places

In an open arrangement, the plumber publishes a service listing under an identity they control. A local directory indexes it. A specialist directory for old buildings indexes it too. A neighbourhood association includes it on a community board.

Each service can offer a different view. One checks qualifications. One knows the local area. One is particularly good at matching urgent jobs with available people.

The listing has an original home, and the directories point back to it. The customer can contact the business through an open messaging system. A quote can be shared directly between the people doing the work.

The same pattern could support writing, research, events and public discussion. An author keeps the original work; different services help readers find it. A club publishes its events; a town calendar and a specialist community make them easier to discover.

This is the direction of Poweur's planned public feeds and indexers. It is roadmap work, not a description of a directory network already running on Poweur.

## Some services need a wide view

A peer-to-peer internet still needs help answering questions such as “What's happening near me?” or “Who has written something useful about this subject?”

Nobody should have to query every person's device to find a bicycle for sale.

An indexer gathers opted-in public information and organises it for search or discovery. It might run a substantial database, cache results and rank them. An aggregator might assemble a readable feed from many sources. These can be central services within an otherwise distributed system.

The important design question is what happens when you stop using one.

Can you still reach the people you met? Can you still read the original material? Can another service build a competing view? Can publishers participate without handing over their private contacts and working documents?

If those answers are yes, aggregation can be a convenience you choose. You might use only a handful of large indexes in daily life, while keeping the option of a smaller service that understands your interests better.

## Small businesses can have a real job here

A community directory does not need to win the entire internet to be useful.

It might need to know a city well, answer the phone and remove fraudulent listings quickly. A professional association might understand which credentials matter in its field. A research community might care about sources and context more than popularity.

Those are real services. They could be supported by memberships, subscriptions, clearly labelled advertising or fees for verification and support. Different communities would make different choices about how to pay for them.

Open protocols do not make operating costs disappear. Search infrastructure, moderation and customer service require money and attention. A smaller service also cannot casually reproduce a global search engine's coverage. Its advantage may come from a narrower scope and better judgement within it.

That seems like a healthier opportunity for a small business: do a particular job well, serve a community, and charge for the value provided.

A hosting provider could make reliable storage its business. A directory could make useful introductions its business. An application developer could make an excellent interface. They would not all need to own the identity system in order to participate.

## An open index still has responsibilities

Separating discovery from ownership doesn't remove the hard problems.

A signed listing tells you which key signed it. It does not prove the plumber is competent. A review attached to an identity does not automatically become honest. Directories still need ways to investigate abuse, judge evidence and explain their decisions.

They also need editorial freedom. A neighbourhood board should be able to remove a scam. A specialist index should be able to exclude irrelevant material. Keeping your original data does not entitle you to appear in somebody else's recommendations.

Public data raises another issue: copies persist. An indexer should honour corrections, deletion notices and withdrawal from indexing, but no design can guarantee that everyone who once fetched a public document will erase it. People need to understand that before publishing.

For this model to remain open in practice, publishers need understandable formats and clients need workable alternatives. A nominally replaceable index that every application quietly hard-codes has become a gatekeeper again. The cost of switching matters as much as the permission to switch.

## Keep the relationship after the introduction

The useful test is a small one.

Suppose you found a local business, hired it and want to work with it again next year. Should that relationship survive the directory closing down?

I think it should. Your conversations and documents should have homes beyond the service that introduced you. The business should be reachable through its own identity. Another directory should be able to help you discover someone else without asking you to rebuild your online life.

That is the role I want indexers and aggregators to play above an open network: help us find things, help us make sense of them, and earn our continued use.

The directory deserves credit for the introduction. The relationship belongs to the people who made it.
